History · The metaphors

From menus to conversations

Every generation of technology brought a new metaphor for what “interacting” means — and each one redrew the limits of what was possible.

We are no longer only telling machines what to do. We are negotiating, collaborating — and trusting them to act on our behalf.
01

Command line · the age of syntax

The human learns the machine’s language. Precise and powerful, but unforgiving — mastery is the price of entry.

02

GUI · the age of visual objects

Desktops, folders, windows: pointing and dragging replace memorising. Computing becomes a household technology.

03

UCD → HCD · the age of experience

From an operator measured by speed and accuracy to a person with emotions, values and context. The computer becomes a mediator of meaning.

04

Agents · the age of mediation

The machine interprets intent and acts on our behalf. Control gives way to coordination: dialogue and co-agency.

IT Key Experts Hour · Agentic UX Patterns · 27 Oct 2026
The story of HCI is a story of metaphors. CLI: humans spoke the machine’s language. GUI: humans acted on representations of machine functions. HCD: systems adapt to human emotions and values. Agents: humans and machines meet in collaborative dialogue and co-agency. Computing is no longer only about control — it is about coordination. That is the bridge to the next slide: from user-centred to human-agent centred design.

History · The legacy mental model

Built for a world where users performed every step

Direct manipulation

Shneiderman: meaningful action happens through visible controls the user operates directly.

User flows

NN/g: a goal is reached through stable, predictable state transitions that can be drawn in advance.

Task analysis

Observes work as sequential units a user performs, then optimises each unit.

These methods stay valuable for research. But as maps of the product they risk becoming historical records of how work used to happen.

In agentic systems, the user’s job is to articulate intention.

IT Key Experts Hour · Agentic UX Patterns · 27 Oct 2026
Where did the flow-as-sequence model come from? Three HCI doctrines, each with its own illustration, building in one after another. Direct manipulation, from Shneiderman: meaningful action happens through visible controls the user operates — drag the thing, see it move. User flows, in the NN/g sense: goals are reached through stable state transitions you can draw before anyone uses the product. Task analysis: watch work as a sequence of units and optimise each one. All three assume the same thing — a human performs every step. That assumption is what agents break. Be careful with the tone here: the article does not reject these methods, and neither should we. They remain excellent research instruments for understanding how users work today. What changes is their status as maps of the product — a flow diagram of a process the agent now runs is a historical record of how the work used to be done. The closing line reframes the user’s role: when the agent performs the steps, the human’s job becomes articulating intention well. Everything from here on follows from that sentence.

01 · How agents work · Context window and memory

Context is what it sees now. Memory is what it keeps.

Context window · what it sees now

One run’s working memory. When it fills, something gives — each option a UX decision made in a config file:

  • Truncation — latest kept, early constraints lost
  • Summarisation — condensed, nuance lost
  • Retrieval — from memory; stale or conflicting
  • Compression — fewer tokens, distorted meaning

Memory · what it keeps between runs

Five stores — working, retrieval, semantic, procedural, durable. Ask each: what it keeps, where, for how long, and who controls it.

When the window fills, what gets cut from the user’s experience — and who decided?
IT Key Experts Hour · Agentic UX Patterns · 27 Oct 2026
Two things that look alike from the outside. The context window is what the agent can see in this run; memory is what it carries between runs. When the context window fills, something gets truncated, summarised, retrieved or compressed — each strategy is a UX decision someone made in a config file. The engineer’s “keep it flexible” was lost exactly here: truncated out of the window and never written to durable memory. The figure lists the five memory stores and the four questions to ask of each; the closing line of the source — memory is your control plane — is the point: if you cannot answer why the agent decided, what data influenced it and where it is recorded, you do not control the product. 02 · Intention mapping · What an intention is

An intention sits between what a person says and what a machine runs

Human language

“Book me Munich, Tuesday, customer site visit.”

Open-ended, under-specified, full of unstated context.

Intention · The job

Arrive rested · be near the customer · keep the option to move

Direction and purpose, with success criteria and context — structured enough for an agent to recognise, interpret and act on.

Agent workflow

Search → hold → verify against the job → ask → book

Rigid, executable steps and decisions.

Jobs articulate human intentions. JTBD already carries goals, success criteria and context, so no extra translation layer is needed — the job map is the intention map.

Agents operationalise them. They interpret the intention and turn it into workflows and decisions — and that translation becomes explicit and traceable.

02 · Workflow encapsulation

Encapsulation: when agents collapse steps

  • the intention the workflow serves
  • the agent’s authority — what it may do alone
  • the guardrails — what it may never do
  • how humans observe, override and audit

Complex operations get bundled and hidden. In Human-Agent Centered Design this is a core design lever: agents mediate intent, negotiate ambiguity and collapse procedural pathways.

Tool encapsulation

The tool’s mechanisms still exist, but are no longer exposed as discrete operations the user performs.

Workflow encapsulation

A whole multi-step procedure — configure, validate, update, notify, document — becomes hidden orchestration. The human supplies intent and sees the result.

IT Key Experts Hour · Agentic UX Patterns · 27 Oct 2026
Encapsulation is the force doing the damage to the flow diagram, so name it precisely. Engineers in the room know the word from software: hide the implementation, expose an interface. The same thing is now happening to user experience. The illustration shows five exposed steps on the upper tier — each one a screen in the old flow — sinking along the dashed guides into a single glowing capsule on the lower tier that takes an intention in on the left and returns a result on the right. The guides are the point: the steps did not vanish, they moved inside. Two levels. Tool encapsulation: the mechanisms of a tool still exist but stop being discrete operations the user has to perform — think of the dozen settings an export dialog used to expose. Workflow encapsulation: an entire procedure — configure, validate, update, notify, document — becomes hidden orchestration, and the human only supplies intent and sees the outcome. In H-ACD terms this is a design lever, not an accident: agents mediate intent, negotiate ambiguity and collapse procedural pathways. Connect to the orchestration deck for anyone who attended: that capsule is exactly the orchestration layer we spent an hour on.

What matters — not everything

05 · Evaluate · Why evaluation

Observability gives you visibility. Evaluation gives you judgment.

Observability asks: what happened?

Traces, logs, tool calls, latencies, token counts. Teams are flooded with signals — and still cannot answer whether the result was good.

Evaluation asks: was that good?

It defines “good” up front, grades behaviour against it, and turns signals into judgment — and judgment into governance.

Without evals, teams live in a reactive loop: wait for complaints, reproduce, fix, and hope nothing else regressed.

IT Key Experts Hour · Agentic UX Patterns · 27 Oct 2026
Both sources land on the same stance from different directions: Anthropic from engineering practice, design@tive from product and UX. Observability is necessary but not sufficient. The reactive loop is what most teams are in today.

05 · Evaluate · Anatomy of an eval

Eight words every eval conversation needs

Task

One test case: defined inputs and success criteria.

Trial

One attempt at a task. Run several — outputs vary.

Grader

Logic that scores one aspect of performance. A task can have many, each with several assertions.

Transcript

The full record of a trial: outputs, tool calls, reasoning, intermediate results. Also: trace, trajectory.

Outcome

The final state of the environment — the reservation that exists, not the one the agent claims.

Evaluation harness

Runs evals end to end: instructions, tools, concurrency, recording, grading, aggregation.

Agent harness

The scaffold that lets a model act as an agent. “The agent” under test is harness plus model.

Evaluation suite

A collection of tasks measuring specific capabilities or behaviours.

IT Key Experts Hour · Agentic UX Patterns · 27 Oct 2026
Vocabulary first — most cross-functional eval arguments are really vocabulary arguments. Stress the two harnesses: the evaluation harness runs the test; the agent harness is part of what is being tested.

05 · Evaluate · Anatomy of an eval

Three families of grader — and what each is for

Code-based

Examples

String matching, fail-to-pass tests, static analysis, outcome and tool-call verification

Strengths

Fast, cheap, objective, reproducible, easy to debug

Weaknesses

Brittle to valid variations; no nuance; weak on subjective tasks

Model-based

Examples

Rubric scoring, natural-language assertions, pairwise comparison, multi-judge consensus

Strengths

Flexible, scalable, captures nuance and open-ended output

Weaknesses

Non-deterministic, costlier, must be calibrated against human graders

Human

Examples

Expert review, spot-check sampling, A/B tests, inter-annotator agreement

Strengths

Gold standard; matches expert judgment; calibrates the model graders

Weaknesses

Expensive, slow, needs experts at scale

Rule of thumb: prefer deterministic graders, use model graders where you must, use humans to validate both.

05 · Evaluate · Judging behaviour

Agents rarely fail abruptly. They drift.

Local optimisation

Each step looks reasonable on its own; the sum of them is not what was asked.

Context loss

Earlier constraints and preferences quietly fall out of the working context.

Ambiguous objectives

The goal was never pinned down, so the agent picks an interpretation and commits.

Reinforcing feedback

Wrong signals get rewarded, repeated and amplified across runs.

A drifting agent can pass every output check and satisfy an LLM judge — while no longer doing what the user asked.

Evaluation as alignment tracking

Beyond accuracy, latency and completion, measure alignment across steps, consistency of decisions, and deviation from the expected trajectory.

IT Key Experts Hour · Agentic UX Patterns · 27 Oct 2026
Drift is the strongest argument for evaluating trajectories rather than outputs. Every one of the four causes is invisible at the level of a single response.

A more complete evaluation model: five questions, three levels

1

What was the intended job or outcome?

2

How was that intent translated into agent goals?

3

How did the agent act across steps and decisions?

4

Where did alignment hold — and where did it break?

5

How does the final outcome compare to the intent?

Interaction level

Quality of a single response or exchange

Workflow level

Task success and friction across the whole sequence of steps

System level

Alignment with intent over time, across many runs and users

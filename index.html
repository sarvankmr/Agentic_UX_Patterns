Part 1 · How agents work

From answering to acting

An assistant replies once. An agent runs a loop.

Assistant

You ask, it answers. One turn, no side effects. You do the work with the answer.

Agent

You state a goal. It perceives the situation, plans steps, acts through tools, verifies the result, and repeats until done or stopped.

Why it matters for design

Every pass of the loop can change something in the world. Each step needs a place where a person can see it, question it or stop it.

[00:07] The loop. Perceive the situation, plan the next step, act through a tool, verify the result, and go round again. The thing to hold on to: every pass can change the world, so every pass needs a surface where a person can see, question or stop it. That is the whole reason patterns exist.

Part 2 · Principles and guardrails

Human-Agent Centered Design

Three parties, one contract

Human

Owns the goal and the values. Decides what good looks like and what must never happen.

Agent

Translates intent into action with bounded autonomy. It does not own goals or judgment; it operationalises them.

System · this room

Reliability, permissions, traceability, scale. Agents bolted onto infrastructure never built for mediation fail faster than they add value.

Binding values

co-agency · transparency · trust as an operating condition · clarity

[00:13] Human-Agent Centered Design puts three parties in a contract. The human owns goals and values. The agent translates and executes with bounded autonomy; it operationalises judgment, it does not own it. The system is reliability, permissions, traceability and scale, which is this room. The four values that bind the contract: co-agency, transparency, trust as an operating condition, clarity.

Part 2 · Principles and guardrails

Principles for trustworthy collaboration

Eight questions the interface must answer

1 · Interrogable reasoning

Why this? Why not that? What did it assume?

2 · Shaped dialogue

Does it understand the job, and say when it cannot act?

3 · Human control

Can I see it, change it, undo it and dial it down?

4 · Feedback loops

Does my correction change what happens next, visibly?

5 · Inclusive and accessible

Can everyone question it, and feel entitled to?

6 · Exposed state

What is it doing now, what is pending, what can I still undo?

7 · Curated awareness

Do I hear about what matters, and only that?

8 · Traceable accountability

Who decided, with what authority, on what information?

Approval is not judgment. Visibility is not control. Presence is not accountability.Beyond the conversation trap · Human-Agent Centered Design series
[00:15] Eight principles, phrased as the question a person is silently asking the interface. The first six come from the article on trustworthy collaboration; seven and eight were added once agents started working alongside direct manipulation and each other. The quote is the test for every screen you will see in the demo: a button is not a judgment, a log is not control, a human nearby is not accountability.

Part 2 · Principles and guardrails

Preserving human control

Autonomy is a dial, not a switch

Advisor

Suggests three flights. You book.

Co-pilot

Drafts the whole trip. Books only after your OK.

Autopilot

Books within policy, shows you the log, pauses when a guardrail trips.

The real risk is not the autonomous agent. It is unobservable delegation: a "smart mode" toggle that is quietly autopilot.

[00:16] Control is a dial with named positions. Advisor suggests, co-pilot drafts and waits for your OK, autopilot acts within guardrails and shows the log. The rule: start low and earn the right to move up. The warning: the dangerous product is not the one on autopilot; it is the one where you cannot tell which position the dial is in.

Part 2 · Principles and guardrails

Guardrails

A guardrail is a boundary with a trigger and a person attached

Confidence above 90 %

Act

Aisle seat, as always. The agent just does it and logs it.

Confidence 60 to 90 %

Explain, then ask

"Your meetings moved twice last quarter. Refundable fare for €40 more?"

Below 60 %, or a policy line

Stop and escalate

Hotel above the nightly cap. Nothing is booked; the case goes to your manager.

Not designed until it has all five

boundarytriggerhuman rolemetricescalation path

"Never pay above policy" is a boundary. The guardrail is: total exceeds the cap (trigger) → pause, nothing charged (action) → manager decides (role) → override rate (metric).

Four boundaries every workflow defines

Act without asking

Act, but explain

Defer: ask first

Stop

[00:18] What a guardrail is, in one slide. Three bands: when the agent is sure, it acts and logs. When it is fairly sure, it explains and asks. When it is unsure, or a policy line is in the way, it stops and escalates. And the rule from the ethics article: a principle is not a guardrail until it has a boundary, a trigger, a human role, a metric and an escalation path. Keep the five words; you will see each one in the demo.

Moment 8 · Confession and audit log

Let the agent report on itself, and keep the record straight

A confession is the agent's own account of shortcuts and uncertainty. The audit log is the immutable record: actor, action, object, time, policy.

What each discipline owns in an agentic product

Data science

Calibration and evaluation

  • Confidence bands tied to measured accuracy, not vibes
  • Evaluate agent outcomes and human outcomes together
  • Counter-evidence that is real, not decorative
  • Escalation precision and recall as a classification problem

DevOps and platform

The harness at runtime

  • Pause, stop and rollback as platform capabilities, not UI promises
  • Discrete step state the interface can show truthfully
  • Escalation routing with max retries and hop counts
  • Observability of intent → action → outcome

IT infrastructure

Rights and records

  • Permission tiers; scope and expiry enforced server-side
  • Environment as a first-class dimension
  • Agents never exceed the invoking human
  • Immutable audit events, redacted, exportable

Digital transformation

Delegation contracts

  • Per workflow: what runs unattended, what needs approval, what is forbidden
  • Adoption path: advisor → co-pilot → autopilot, earned
  • The rule: no boundary, trigger, role, metric and escalation path means not designed yet
  • Dual evaluation before scaling a pilot
